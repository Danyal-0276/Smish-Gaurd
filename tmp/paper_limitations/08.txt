ABSTRACT
Three recurrent problems have emerged on short-claim fake-news detection: am-
biguous label mappings, unfair comparison with the long-article settings, and
ill-defined contributions of affective features. We tackle these three issues using
Multi-Transformer Sentimental LIAR: a controlled paradigm in which we compare
RoBERTa, ELECTRA, BERT and DistilBERT on official LIAR partitions under
one publicly revealed clear-label scheme. Text-only, sentiment, emotion, and joint
affectiveness setting were measured and compared for its accuracy, precision, recall,
F1 score, AUC of receiver operating characteristic curve, Matthew’s correlation
coefficient, model size, training time respectively. With joint affective input ELEC-
TRA has reached an accuracy of72.03%, an F1 score of 0.7626, and a Matthews
correlation coefficient of 0.4247.However, with solely text input, ELECTRA reached
82.03%accuracy, 0.8356 for F1 score and 0.6404 for Matthews correlation coefficient.
It proves that affectives augmentation is context-aware depending on encoder’s ar-
chitecture and sometimes can negatively affect its performance. DistilBERT obtains
70.76% accuracy with 66.96 Million parameters, a suitable deployment model. A
traceable claim screening is made possible via the reported method, well documented
algorithm, Gradio UI, the result is kept as support for human judgment.

1. Introduction
False and misleading claims burden journalism, platform moderation, public communi-
cation, and political decision-making because harmful content can circulate faster than
complete fact-checking reports ( 1–3, 25). Short claims are particularly difficult because
they provide little context, contain weak discourse structure, and may use ambiguous or
emotionally persuasive language ( 4–6). An automated classifier cannot establish factual
truth by itself, but it can support early screening by ranking suspicious claims for human
review.

Earlier studies demonstrate how strongly task definition affects performance. The
original LIAR benchmark reported 27.4% accuracy, subsequent speaker-profile and meta-
data extensions reported 41.5% and 45.7%, Sentimental LIAR reported approximately

Hypotheses..We test three hypotheses. First, matched encoders will produce different
error profiles because their pre-training objectives differ. Second, sentiment and emotion
features will not improve every encoder because they can duplicate contextual information
or introduce noisy tokens. Third, DistilBERT will retain sufficient predictive performance

2. Literature Review
Fake-news detection is commonly understood as an inference problem built from three
broad evidence families: the linguistic content of an item, the social context in which
it is produced and propagated, and external knowledge used to verify its claims ( 1–
3). Content-based methods examine lexical choice, syntax, semantics, writing style,
and rhetorical framing, whereas context-based methods use information such as source
credibility, user engagement, and diffusion structure (1, 2). Knowledge-based approaches
instead compare a statement with trusted evidence or structured facts ( 2, 3). Evidence-
grounded benchmarks such as FEVER make this distinction explicit by pairing supported
or refuted decisions with textual evidence, rather than inferring veracity from claim
wording alone (21). These families are complementary, but they are not interchangeable.
Because LIAR consists of short political statements rather than complete articles or
propagation graphs, the present study focuses on content-derived evidence and treats its

2.1. Short-claim fake news and the LIAR benchmark
Wang introduced LIAR as a short political-claim dataset with six truthfulness grades
and speaker metadata. The original hybrid convolutional neural network achieved 27.4%
accuracy, illustrating the difficulty of six-class claim classification ( 4). Later speaker-
profile and metadata methods reported 41.5% and 45.7% accuracy, while a hard binary
benchmark reported approximately 62% ( 5, 6). These improvements are important, but
differences in label conversion and feature availability prevent direct ranking. This study
addresses that limitation by disclosing one binary mapping and evaluating every model
on the same filtered partitions.
2.2. Affective augmentation theory
Sentiment and emotion describe rhetorical tone, but they do not provide external evidence
that a proposition is true or false (12, 13). Prior linguistic analysis has identified stylistic
and affective differences across unreliable news and political fact-checking categories,
while FakeFlow models changes in affective information across a document ( 26, 30).
Sentimental LIAR combined Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers
with affective and speaker-credit features and reported approximately 70% accuracy ( 5).
These results motivate affect-aware modeling, but they do not establish that sentiment
and emotion improve every transformer architecture. The unresolved gap is feature
necessity: affective information must be compared with a text-only baseline under
identical data and evaluation conditions. This study fills that gap through four controlled
feature settings for every encoder.
2.3. Explainability and multi-transformer ensembles
The four selected transformer encoders use different learning strategies: bidirectional
masked-token learning, optimized pre-training, replaced-token discrimination, and knowl-
edge distillation (8–11). Previous single-model studies cannot show whether a reported
gain arises from the feature design or the selected encoder. This study therefore uses a
matched four-model comparison and reports accuracy, precision, recall, F1 score, area
under the receiver operating characteristic curve, and Matthews correlation coefficient
rather than relying on accuracy alone.
2.4. Deployment and responsible use
Interactive ML demos (Gradio) provide standardized claim-entry forms with metadata for
model choice and affective diagnostics ( 17). Those interfaces answer a different scientific
question,can a non-expert run the model?, from the notebook question,what Acc did
seed 42 yield?Transparent release should additionally state intended use, evaluation
conditions, and limitations, following the model-card principle for documented ML
deployment (23). Multi-Transformer Sentimental LIAR therefore uses Gradio only as
deployment context: compulsory claim text, optional affective display, and DistilBERT
as the efficiency default.

2.5. Research gap
Prior work leaves three linked limitations: inconsistent binary-label protocols, invalid
comparison between short claims and long articles, and insufficient matched ablation
of affective features across transformer architectures. We address these gaps with one
disclosed LIAR label mapping, strict separation of article and claim results, and four

3. Methodology
The central problem addressed by this work is the absence of a scientifically honest
short-claim decision-support stack that can:
(1) Accept routine claim text required for fact-check screening;
(2) Produce false or real screening scores from matched transformers without mixing
long-article accuracy with short-claim accuracy; and
(3) Determine whether sentiment and emotion descriptors improve performance beyond
claim text alone.

3.1. Framework design
Framework design explains how the scientific components operate as one controlled
system. It connects the prediction task, label definition, feature construction, transformer
encoders, ablation conditions, evaluation rules, and deployment criterion. The design
keeps the source data and target labels fixed while changing only the feature setting
and encoder. This isolation is essential because an observed performance difference
can otherwise result from a changed dataset, a different label mapping, or a different
test partition rather than from the model itself. The framework therefore supports two
comparisons: an architectural comparison among four encoders and a feature comparison
among four input settings. Both comparisons use the same official partitions and the
same binary task.
The framework also separates predictive modeling from factual verification. It learns
statistical associations from short claim text and optional affective descriptors, but
it does not retrieve supporting documents or validate a proposition against external
evidence. Evidence-aware systems such as DeClarE address a broader verification setting
by combining claims with retrieved evidence ( 28). In contrast, the present framework
is designed for early screening, controlled experimentation, and transparent model
comparison. This boundary determines how the results and the Gradio output should
be interpreted.

3.1.4. Model selection rationale
The four encoders were selected because they represent complementary solutions to
limitations observed in earlier text classifiers. Classical bag-of-words and shallow neural
models have difficulty representing context, word order, negation, and long-range rela-
tions within a claim. Transformer self-attention supplies the shared contextual foundation
for the selected encoders ( 14). Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers
addresses this problem through bidirectional contextual pre-training, but its masked-
language objective and computational cost do not guarantee the best short-claim decision
boundary (8). Robustly Optimized Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Trans-
formers improves the pre-training recipe and can strengthen contextual representations,
although its larger parameter count increases resource demand ( 9). Efficiently Learn-
ing an Encoder that Classifies Token Replacements Accurately uses replaced-token
discrimination, which provides a learning signal at more token positions and may be
advantageous when claims are short ( 11). Distilled Bidirectional Encoder Represen-
tations from Transformers transfers knowledge from a larger teacher into a smaller
model, making it suitable for interactive deployment, although compression can reduce
predictive capacity (10). Evaluating all four models under matched conditions reveals
whether performance differences arise from architecture rather than from inconsistent
datasets or label rules.

4. Experimental Setup
The experimental setup converts the methodology into a reproducible evaluation proce-
dure. The methodology defines the data, labels, features, and model family; this section
defines how the models are trained, how decisions are selected, and how performance
is measured. All four encoders use the same retained claims and official partitions.
The comparison therefore changes the encoder or feature setting without changing the
underlying test population. This separation reduces confounding and ensures that later
differences can be interpreted as model or feature effects rather than as consequences of
inconsistent data preparation.
The setup follows a strict training, validation, and testing sequence. Training data
estimate model parameters. Validation data control early stopping and decision-threshold
selection. Test data remain isolated until the final evaluation. The reported run uses
seed 42 so that its outputs can be reproduced, but a single seed cannot estimate
optimization variance. Repeated runs and complete configuration reporting remain
necessary for stronger reproducibility claims (24,32).
4.1. Training protocol
All encoders use the same filtered LIAR partitions and a common transformer fine-tuning
workflow (15). Training uses class-weighted loss, early stopping, and threshold selection
on the validation partition. The test partition remains untouched until final evaluation.

4.4. Probability calibration
The models produce scores for ranking and thresholding. These scores are not automati-
cally calibrated probabilities of truth. Calibration should be measured on held-out data
with reliability diagrams, expected calibration error, the Brier score, and calibration-
sensitive comparison of learning algorithms before an interface displays probability
language ( 22, 36, 37). The current application presents model scores as screening
outputs and avoids interpreting them as verified confidence.

5.3. Ablation and error analysis
The ablation analysis tests whether sentiment and emotion add useful information beyond
the claim text already encoded by each transformer. It changes one feature condition at
a time while preserving the label protocol and data partitions. This controlled design
distinguishes a genuine feature contribution from a gain caused by a different dataset or

The three measures answer different questions. Accuracy measures total correctness
but can conceal class preference. The F1 score emphasizes the precision and recall
balance for the positive class but excludes true negatives. The Matthews correlation
coefficient incorporates the complete confusion matrix and exposes degenerate behavior.
This distinction is essential for the ELECTRA emotion-only condition: its accuracy
appears non-trivial, but its zero Matthews correlation coefficient indicates that the
predictions contain no useful balanced association with the observed labels.

5.5. Qualitative error analysis
Short claims fail for identifiable linguistic reasons. Numerical claims often omit a date,
denominator, or comparison group. Negation can reverse a claim while preserving most of
its vocabulary. Entity substitutions can make a linguistically plausible sentence factually
wrong. Quoted speech can also cause the model to attribute a statement to the wrong
speaker. Affective tags do not resolve these problems because none supplies external
evidence.
Representative misclassification patterns.Table 9 records concrete examples for
qualitative audit. The aggregate result package does not retain the claim-level text
and paired predictions needed to assert that these exact sentences were test-set errors.
The table therefore presents representative error patterns rather than fabricated test
instances.
5.6. Why text-only ELECTRA performs best
Results show that ELECTRA performs best without appended affective tags. Three
mechanisms explain this outcome. First, replaced-token discrimination already trains
ELECTRA to detect locally inconsistent wording, which is valuable for short claims (11).
Second, sentiment and emotion descriptors repeat information that the contextual encoder
can infer from the original text. This redundancy consumes sequence capacity without
adding factual evidence. Third, serialized tags alter token positions and attention patterns.
Noisy or weakly relevant emotion tokens can therefore shift the decision boundary away
from the claim itself. The decline from 82.03% text-only accuracy to 72.03% joint affective
accuracy supports this explanation. The result does not show that affect is always useless.
It shows that feature value depends on the encoder, task, and serialization method.

5.7. Literature and capability comparison
Tables 10 to 11 compare the framework with representative short-claim, affect-aware,
transformer, and long-article systems ( 5–7). The comparison serves two purposes. First,
it places the reported accuracy values within their correct task definitions. A six-class
LIAR result, a binary LIAR result, and a long-article result use different target spaces and
evidence conditions. Their numerical values cannot support a direct universal ranking.
Second, the comparison examines methodological coverage. It asks whether each system
discloses its label protocol, evaluates several encoders under matched conditions, tests
affective features through ablation, reports balanced measures such as the Matthews
correlation coefficient and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve,
and provides an accessible application.
Table 10 therefore emphasizes construct validity rather than numerical competition.
Sentimental LIAR provides the closest affect-aware comparison, but it uses a different
label conversion. The Khan benchmark provides a difficult binary reference, while
WELFake demonstrates the higher performance achievable when complete articles
supply more linguistic evidence. The transformer rows show that using a strong encoder
alone does not guarantee a fair comparison. Dataset control, label disclosure, feature
control, and consistent evaluation remain necessary.
Table 11 extends the analysis beyond accuracy. The proposed framework combines
four elements that earlier pipelines usually address separately: matched transformer
evaluation, explicit affective ablation, multi-measure reporting, and Gradio deployment.
This combination supports an integration contribution. It does not claim that the
individual encoders, sentiment lexicon, emotion lexicon, or interface technology are
independently novel.

one selected operating point. This analysis matters because accuracy, precision, recall,
F1 score, and Matthews correlation coefficient depend on a particular threshold. Two
models can produce similar threshold-specific scores while ranking claims differently
across the full score range.

6. Discussion
6.1. Scientific interpretation
Matched multi-transformer evaluation reveals errors that a single-model accuracy score
would hide (5, 6). ELECTRA achieves 72.03% accuracy with joint affective information
and 82.03% with text alone. Sentimental LIAR reports approximately 70% under a
different label mapping, while the higher WELFake result concerns long articles ( 5, 7).
Keeping these tasks separate prevents an invalid comparison.
6.2. Theoretical implications of affective augmentation
Affective features can help when lexical sentiment captures rhetorical framing that the
encoder underrepresents. They can hurt when the tags duplicate contextual information,
exaggerate emotionally charged but truthful language, or introduce tokens unrelated to
factuality (5, 12, 13). The results support the second hypothesis: feature value depends
on the encoder. RoBERTa gains slightly in selected emotion-based conditions, whereas
ELECTRA and BERT perform best with text alone. The evidence therefore rejects a
universal claim that sentiment and emotion improve fake-news detection.
6.3. Operational implications
Model choice depends on the screening objective. ELECTRA provides the strongest over-
all discrimination. RoBERTa provides the highest recall and suits settings where missed
target claims carry the greatest cost. DistilBERT provides the strongest resource profile
for interactive use. These roles should remain separate because analytical leadership,
recall-oriented screening, and deployment efficiency optimize different objectives.
6.4. Interpretation of the hypotheses
The results support the first hypothesis because the encoders produce different metric
and error profiles under matched conditions. They support the second hypothesis because
affective augmentation helps some configurations but reduces ELECTRA performance.
The deployment evidence supports the third hypothesis because DistilBERT retains

7. Deployment
Deployment translates the evaluated pipeline into an accessible inference workflow
without changing the training data, evaluation protocol, or reported test results. Gradio
exposes the selected DistilBERT model and affective diagnostics, while model-card-style
documentation states the intended use, operating conditions, and limitations ( 17, 23).
The classifier neither retrieves evidence nor produces calibrated truth probabilities. Its
output supports screening and prioritization rather than final fact-checking (21,22).
7.1. Model packaging
The trained model is packaged with its label protocol, feature setting, intended screening
use, evaluation measures, and limitations. This documentation follows the core model-
card principle of making operating conditions explicit (23).
7.2. Gradio interface
The Gradio application requires claim text and returns a model label, a screening score,
and affective diagnostics ( 17). Figure 8 shows the interface layout used to organize
claim entry, DistilBERT inference, screening output, affective diagnostics, and the
responsible-use warning.

7.3. End-to-end deployment process
The deployment process follows the experimental sequence. The system filters LIAR
claims with the clear-label rule, computes affective descriptors, applies the selected
transformer, and displays the result for human review. Model selection considers predic-
tive performance and computational cost together. The interface does not convert the
classifier into an evidence-retrieval or truth-verification system.
8. Limitations and Future Work
This section defines the boundary of the reported evidence and converts each limitation
into a concrete research direction. The results establish performance for one clear-label
conversion of English political claims, one fixed random seed, and one in-domain test
partition. They do not establish stability across repeated fine-tuning runs, calibrated
probabilities, fairness across demographic or political subgroups, or generalization to
other topics and languages. The deployed interface also remains a screening tool because
the model does not retrieve evidence or verify claims against primary sources. Stating
these limits prevents the strong in-domain results from being interpreted as universal
fact-checking performance.
8.1. Single-seed and statistical uncertainty
The reported experiment uses one fixed seed. This design makes the run reproducible
but does not quantify variation across random initializations ( 24, 32). Future work
should repeat every model and feature setting across several seeds and report the mean,
standard deviation, confidence interval, and rank stability. The next evaluation should
also retain paired claim-level predictions so that McNemar tests can compare models
without reconstructing invalid statistics from aggregate confusion matrices (35).
8.2. Calibration and subgroup evaluation
The study does not evaluate probability calibration or subgroup performance. Interface
scores must therefore not be interpreted as calibrated probabilities of truth ( 22, 23).
Future experiments should report reliability diagrams, expected calibration error, Brier
score, and calibration before and after temperature scaling ( 36, 37). They should also
report performance across speakers, topics, parties, and time periods when ethically
and statistically appropriate. Dataset and model documentation should record these
evaluations so that deployment conditions remain visible (23,34).
8.3. Cross-domain generalizability
LIAR contains English political claims from one fact-checking context. Performance can
change for health misinformation, disaster rumors, financial claims, other countries, other
languages, or complete news articles. The present results therefore establish in-domain
short-claim performance only. Future work should test external claim datasets such as
MultiFC, conduct temporal validation, and evaluate domain adaptation before deploy-
ment outside the LIAR setting ( 27, 29, 31). It should also measure performance under
explicit distribution shifts because pretrained transformers can lose accuracy outside the

training distribution, and compression does not guarantee equal robustness (38). A struc-
tured benchmark design similar to WILDS would help separate temporal, geographic,
topical, and source shifts instead of reporting one combined external score (39).
8.4. Evidence and application limits
The system does not retrieve primary sources, model multimodal evidence, or verify
numerical claims against external databases. Affective tag serialization also provides a
simple auxiliary representation rather than learned multimodal fusion. Future work should
combine claim classification with evidence retrieval and ranking, following evidence-aware
and benchmark-based fact-checking research ( 21, 28, 29). It should then evaluate how
journalists and researchers use the output rather than assuming that interface availability
proves practical benefit (33). Until then, outputs should support human review rather
than replace it (1,2).
8.5. Future research roadmap
Future work should proceed in five stages. First, repeated multi-seed experiments
should quantify optimization uncertainty and test whether encoder rankings remain
stable (24, 32). Second, claim-level prediction records should support McNemar tests,
bootstrap confidence intervals, and transparent error comparisons (35). Third, calibration
experiments should compare raw scores, temperature scaling, reliability diagrams, and
Brier scores before the interface uses probability language ( 22, 36, 37). Fourth, external
evaluation should cover MultiFC, temporal splits, multilingual claims, and controlled
distribution shifts ( 27, 38, 39). Fifth, behavioral test suites should perturb names,
quantities, negation, dates, and emotional wording to reveal model invariances and
brittle shortcuts that aggregate accuracy cannot expose (40).
The longer-term system should integrate retrieval, evidence ranking, verdict predic-
tion, and explanation while preserving the present distinction between screening and
verification (21, 28, 29). Human-centered trials should measure review time, false-
alarm burden, trust calibration, and correction quality for journalists, educators, and
moderation researchers (33). These extensions would transform the current controlled
classifier into a more complete and responsibly evaluated fact-checking assistant without
overstating what claim text alone can establish.
These limitations define the boundary of the contribution and motivate the final
conclusion.
9. Conclusion
Multi-Transformer Sentimental LIAR integrates clear-label LIAR claims, sentiment and
emotion descriptors, four matched transformer encoders, systematic ablation, and a
Gradio application without mixing article and claim accuracy. ELECTRA achieves
72.03% accuracy under the joint affective setting and 82.03% with text alone, demon-
strating that affective augmentation is architecture-dependent. DistilBERT remains
suitable for deployment at 70.76% accuracy with 66.96 million parameters. The study
therefore provides a well-defined path from short-claim data to comparative evaluation
and efficient interactive screening.
